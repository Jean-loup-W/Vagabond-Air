Maison 8 rue Renaudette (15th century)

This residence is one of the five canonical houses existing in the 15th century in the village of Celle-L'Evescault.

In 1904, the house fell into ruins, but certain architectural elements were saved and placed on the garden side to later be reused or restored to their original location by the current owner.

The street-facing facade features openings richly decorated with carved elements. Observe the door and window lintels adorned with ogee arches. This motif, widely used in 15th and 16th-century architecture, displays a curve hinting at the shape of two heels, giving rise to its nickname "oogee arch" (arc en talon).

Inside, a 15th-century fresco depicting the Annunciation adorned the mantle of the fireplace on the first floor. In 1924, it was removed and donated to the Society of Antiquaries of the West for its preservation.

The name of the school recalls the presence of Saint Florence in Celle-L'Evescault. Florence was consecrated to God by Saint Hilary, one of Poitou's first evangelizers in the 4th century. Upon her death, her burial site became a widely renowned place of worship. In the 11th century, a Benedictine priory dependent on the abbey of Nouaillé-Maupertuis was established on the heights of Comblé.

Florence was canonized after her death in 367.

Former oil mill

Celle-L'Evescault had two oil mills intended to extract walnut oil using a press until the 1940s[cite: 12].

The bedstone (meule dormante), which remained stationary during the grinding operation, was formed by a kind of circular gutter in Brittany granite[cite: 12]. The runner stone (meule volante), a massive wheel fixed on a wooden pivot, was pulled by a horse and rolled in this gutter, crushing the whole walnuts placed inside[cite: 12].

A paste was obtained, which was then heated to evaporate moisture[cite: 12]. Finally, cold pressing extracted the oil[cite: 12].

In the past, residents of neighboring villages brought their own walnut production to return home with their jug of oil[cite: 12].

Upstream, numerous evening gatherings were required to crack all these walnuts and remove the kernels from the shell[cite: 12]. Moments of family or friendly reunions, these gatherings allowed a long and repetitive task to be carried out in good spirits[cite: 12].

Celle-l'Evescault Bridge

The Celle-l'Evescault bridge is a major witness of medieval civil architecture in the Melusine region[cite: 14]. It spans the Vonne river to facilitate the connection between Poitiers and Melle[cite: 14].

Dating back to the 13th century, it is said to be built by an "unnamed lord bishop" to allow ecclesiastical personnel in transit to bypass the Lusignan toll[cite: 14].

Built of limestone materials, it is remarkable for its length of about 110 meters[cite: 14]. The width of the roadway between the parapets is 4.50 meters[cite: 14].

Its eight arches—the one on the right bank in a pointed arch (arch formed of two circular arcs), and the seven others in a full-center arch (with a semi-circular vault)—are very irregularly spaced and lean on islets[cite: 14]. The piers are equipped with triangular cutwaters facing upstream[cite: 14].

In the Melusine region during medieval times, there were five bridges[cite: 14]. That of Pranzay (severed by water and time), those of Sanxay, Curzay-sur-Vonne, and Jazeneuil (established on secondary routes connecting Poitiers to Saint-Maixent), and then that of Celle-l'Evescault[cite: 14]. They all allowed the crossing of the Vonne[cite: 14].

The role of bishops during the era of Austrapius

Under Clotaire I, the king was locally represented by the count, who exercised all powers in his name: administrative, judicial, financial, and military[cite: 15].

In the 6th century, Duke Austrapius, former governor of Aquitaine, was made a bishop by King Clotaire[cite: 15]. Besides his mainly military role, he became an intermediary between the king and the count[cite: 15].

While in Roman times the bishop was chosen by the clergy and residents of the civitas from among the wealthiest families, King Clotaire, like all Merovingian rulers, granted himself the right to appoint bishops in order to turn them into public officials[cite: 15].

The role of the episcopate was immense because it fulfilled what the State used to provide: the bishop had to ensure the subsistence of the weak, found hospitals and hospices to welcome them, maintain schools, and very often administer justice[cite: 15].

Who was Gregory of Tours?
Georges Florent Grégoire was born in Clermont-Ferrand around 539 into a noble family related to the most illustrious houses of Gaul[cite: 15]. In 573, Gregory was appointed Bishop of Tours[cite: 15].
Deeply concerned with the propagation of the Christian faith and a witness of the events of his time, he was also the first historian of the Merovingians[cite: 15]. His main work, History of the Franks, is a collection of 10 books telling historical facts relating to Clovis and his successors[cite: 15]. This work earned him his nickname "Father of French History"[cite: 15]. Without him, we would know nothing of the historical events taking place in Celle-l'Evescault throughout the 6th century[cite: 15].
